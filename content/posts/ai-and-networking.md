<h3>AI and Networking</h3>

Bill Gates recently called the development of AI as fundamental of a creation as the microprocessor, the personal computer, the Internet, and the mobile phone. He is most likely right. OpenAI found that around 80% of the U.S. workforce could have at least 10% of their work tasks affected by the introduction of large language models (LLMs). If you are in technology that will be more like 100%. 

What does this mean for networking and the folks in the industry? Before the AI anxiety creeps in and you see the house for a bunker in Montana, the short and medium term impact is the potential to use AI, increase your output and learn new things. Since Twitter has been turned into a dumpster fire as of late, I've paid much more attention to r/networking/ on Reddit. Almost daily there is a network engineer trying to figure out how to transition in the world of automation and software. 

What projects/free-products such as ChatGPT, Bing Chat and Google Bard present today is a generative Stackoverflow, Google, GitHub or a good IDE in one. (I can't believe I just said Bing). Learning to use it in your toolkit will save you time. The generated code is probably slightly worse than you would find in a randos repository on GitHub but the speed to derive that nugget that may unblock you can be impressive. If you are convinced AI will eradicate humans anytime soon, than you haven't seen the bugs it can crank out.

Having been playing with GPT recently it is a marvel from a technological standpoint to have a back and forth with a machine to see if you can feed the prompt enough information to derive a solution or code that most of the time doesn't compile but can be refined through more prompt engineering. Sometimes, I find myself being extra nice to my AI overlord to try and get a better answer... yes, we're doomed. If I just encourage this bot to not be so lazy it can do a better job of self-learning and do what I want it to do. It even gets slightly defensive when you correct it. Angsty AI.. It's your fault my code burned down the plant, not mine, I'm just a machine.

The exciting part is the barrier to entry for many in networking is being intimidated to come out of their day to day ops and architecting because learning automation and/or programming can be daunting, this new tooling can accelerate your learning. Assembly programming was eventually phased out for most to a more productive abstraction. That is what I imagine will occur here. Technology including networking has scaled through layering abstractions, but that is at a critical mass. The stack is getting deeper by the day. New protocols and technologies such as QUIC http/3, EBPF or complex service routing are yet another layer of new complexities. Troubleshooting the stack can shave years off your life. Long story short, AI has the potential for everyone to be a bit of a wizard over time. Even the OPenAI team admitted that some of the more profound results from GPT3/3.5 were not intended and they still don't exactly understand how they occurred. 

I openly admit that thinking of how AI LLMs can be applied to networking and security in infrastructure is pretty damn exciting. Having adaptive agents that can be constantly fuzzing for issues adaptively will change infrastructure and alleviate some of the pressures on us mere mortals trying to build and operate infrastructure. It's something I will be trying to make time to blog on as I explore how these tools can be applied to infra. It's a big deal but it's still a tool, not a replacement. It's a great time to cross train. Having a deep domain expertise in networking and security is not something AI can generate like general purpose coding, so dig in and don't be intimidated.
